Spinal Cord Tracts — UMN or LMN?

Damage to which part of the spinal cord causes LMN signs?

Anterior horn cells are the cell body of the second-order neurons that leave the spinal cord and innervate peripheral muscles. The anterior horn is just a collection of grey matter housing these cells collectively. Damage to the anterior horn cell leads to LMN signs, whilst damage to the descending corticospinal tract results in UMN signs below the lesion.

Tract Positions

The dorsal columns (vibration, proprioception, fine touch) run posterior. The spinothalamic tract (pain, temperature, crude touch) runs anterolateral. The lateral corticospinal tract (limb motor) runs lateral, and the anterior corticospinal tract (trunk, posture) runs anterior.

Two Different Pathways for Touch

Fine touch travels in the dorsal column; crude touch travels in the spinothalamic tract. This means a sensory deficit may still leave some perception of touch — the pathway that isn't damaged carries the signal.

Decussation Matters Clinically

The spinothalamic tract crosses at the level it enters the cord. The dorsal column and corticospinal tracts cross in the medulla. This is the key to Brown-Séquard syndrome: ipsilateral motor + dorsal column loss, but contralateral pain/temperature loss.

UMN vs LMN

Damage to the anterior horn cell → LMN signs (flaccid weakness, wasting, fasciculations). Damage to the descending corticospinal tract → UMN signs below the lesion (spasticity, brisk reflexes, upgoing plantar).

Central Cord Lesions

Pain and temperature are affected first because the spinothalamic tract fibres cross centrally. As the lesion enlarges, other tracts are recruited. Syringomyelia is a classic cause — watch for a cape-distribution sensory loss.

Posterior Cord Pattern

Vibration and proprioception loss with preserved motor and pain/temperature is the signature of posterior cord disease. Remember B12 deficiency and tabes dorsalis (tertiary syphilis) — plus trauma, compression, and MS.
