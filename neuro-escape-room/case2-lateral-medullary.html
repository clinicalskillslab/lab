Stage 3 · MCQ

Cranial Nerve Pearl MCQs

Which of the following is true regarding lesions which affect cranial nerve nuclei in the brainstem?

Cranial Nerve Signs are IPSILATERAL! Just to be clear, we are talking about the nucleus of cranial nerves — not the tracts running up and down the brainstem, which we will come to shortly!
Stage 4 · MCQ

Cranial Nerve Pearl MCQs

Which of the following cranial nerves would be impacted by a lesion in the cervical spinal cord as opposed to the brainstem?

CNs 9, 10, 11 and 12 are located in the medulla. Here CN 11 joins forces with the vagus nerve. However, the function we assess on examination — weakness of sternocleidomastoid and trapezius — localises to the cervical spinal cord. So brainstem medullary lesions don't tend to cause a clinical CN 11 palsy.

Stage 6 · Multi-Select

Decussation and the Brainstem

The ascending and descending tracts have particular points of crossing over (decussation) which are important for our clinical problem solving.

Which two tracts crossover in the medulla of the brainstem? Select all that apply, then check.

Lock that in! The Corticospinal Tract (motor) and Dorsal Column (vibration and proprioception) both decussate in the medulla. The spinothalamic fibres (pain and temperature) cross a couple of segments up from where they enter the spinal cord — so by the time they reach the brainstem they are already crossed, carrying signals from the contralateral body.
Stage 7 · Horner's Syndrome

What is Horner's Syndrome?

You placed the sympathetic tract in the lateral brainstem in Stage 5 — a lesion here will lead to Horner's syndrome. But what does that actually mean?

Let's make sure we know the clinical features — not just for the neuro exam, but for the respiratory and thyroid exams too!

What do you notice about this patient's eyes?

Part A — Tick all that apply

Which of the following are features of Horner's syndrome?

Horner's syndrome = Miosis + Ptosis + Anhidrosis (on the same side as the lesion).

The sympathetic nervous system controls pupil dilation, eyelid elevation and sweating. Damage = the opposite of all three — constriction, drooping, dryness.

Diplopia is not a feature — eye movements are controlled by CN III, IV and VI, not the sympathetic tract. Mydriasis is the opposite — that's sympathetic over-activity (or parasympathetic failure).

Part B — Localisation

In a LEFT lateral medullary lesion, the sympathetic tract is damaged. On which side would Horner's syndrome appear?

Ipsilateral — always! The descending sympathetic fibres travel through the lateral brainstem without crossing at this level. A left-sided lesion damages left-sided sympathetic fibres → left-sided Horner's. This is the same rule as cranial nerve nuclei: lateral brainstem structures produce ipsilateral signs.

Learning Pearls 🧠

Brainstem levels

Midbrain → Pons → Medulla. The cranial nerve nuclei follow: CN 3–4 in midbrain, CN 5–8 in pons, CN 9–12 in medulla.

Ipsilateral cranial nerve signs

A lesion of a CN nucleus in the brainstem always produces ipsilateral cranial nerve signs — the fibres haven't crossed yet.

CN XI exception

CN 11's clinical function (SCM and trapezius) localises to the cervical spinal cord — medullary lesions don't cause CN 11 palsy.

Medial = movement, Lateral = S tracts

Corticospinal (motor) and dorsal columns run medially. Spinothalamic, Spinocerebellar, Spinal Trigeminal and Sympathetic tracts run laterally.

Decussation in the medulla

Corticospinal tract and dorsal columns cross in the medulla. The spinothalamic tract has already crossed (in the spinal cord), so it carries contralateral body signals by the time it reaches the brainstem.

Lateral medullary syndrome (Wallenberg's)

Ipsilateral CN signs (uvula deviation away from lesion, hoarseness), ipsilateral Horner's, ipsilateral cerebellar signs, ipsilateral face pain/temp loss, contralateral body pain/temp loss. No motor weakness or tongue deviation — those are medial structures.
