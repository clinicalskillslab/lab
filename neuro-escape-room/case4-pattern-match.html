Key Learning Point

Internal Capsule (Lacunar Infarcts)

The internal capsule is a deep white matter structure where descending motor fibres and ascending sensory fibres travel to and from the cerebral cortex.


The posterior limb of the internal capsule contains:

  • Corticospinal motor fibres
  • Ascending sensory fibres travelling from the thalamus to the sensory cortex

Because these fibre bundles run very close together, a small infarct may affect:

  • Motor fibres alone → Pure motor stroke
  • Sensory fibres alone → Pure sensory stroke (less common)
  • Both motor and sensory fibres → Pure sensorimotor stroke

No Cortical Signs

Unlike cortical strokes, internal capsule infarcts do not directly damage the cerebral cortex. As a result, cortical signs are absent.


These patients should not have:

  • Aphasia
  • Hemispatial neglect
  • Astereognosis (inability to identify an object by touch)
  • Agraphesthesia (inability to recognise a number or letter traced on the skin)

If any of these cortical features are present, the lesion should instead be localised to the cerebral cortex. For this reason, internal capsule strokes are classically described as subcortical (lacunar) strokes.

Key Learning Point

Thalamic Stroke

The thalamus is the brain's major sensory relay centre, processing and relaying sensory information to the cerebral cortex. Because the thalamus is primarily involved in sensory pathways (rather than the descending corticospinal motor tract), small thalamic infarcts classically produce a pure sensory stroke.


Although the thalamus is an important processing centre, it lies deep within the cerebral hemisphere rather than in the cortex. Therefore, the usual rules for subcortical strokes apply — no aphasia, no hemispatial neglect, no other cortical signs.

Thalamus vs Internal Capsule

So how do we distinguish a thalamic stroke from an internal capsule stroke?


The internal capsule contains tightly packed motor fibres (corticospinal and corticobulbar tracts) together with ascending sensory fibres. Therefore:

  • Pure motor stroke → think internal capsule
  • Sensorimotor stroke → think internal capsule
  • Pure sensory stroke → think thalamus

A pure sensory internal capsule stroke can occur, but it is uncommon. In these cases, the examination alone may not distinguish the lesion, and brain imaging is required.

Cortical vs Subcortical — the Key Distinction

Cortical strokes (MCA territory) produce motor and/or sensory deficits PLUS cortical signs: aphasia (left hemisphere), hemispatial neglect (right hemisphere), astereognosis, or agraphesthesia. Subcortical strokes (lacunar / deep) produce deficits WITHOUT these cortical features.

MCA Lateralisation

Left MCA → right-sided weakness and sensory loss + aphasia (dominant hemisphere). Right MCA → left-sided weakness and sensory loss + hemispatial neglect (non-dominant hemisphere). The face is involved because the corticobulbar fibres also run in MCA territory.

Internal Capsule — Packed Fibres

The posterior limb of the internal capsule packs motor and sensory fibres tightly together — a small infarct here can cause a pure motor, pure sensory, or sensorimotor stroke without any cortical signs. If cortical signs are present, look to the cortex instead.

Thalamic Stroke — Pure Sensory

The thalamus relays sensory signals but is not in the motor pathway, so thalamic infarcts produce pure sensory strokes. Like internal capsule strokes, cortical signs are absent. Motor-dominant or sensorimotor deficits should steer you toward the internal capsule rather than the thalamus.

The Decoys — Why They Matter

Recognising what doesn't fit is as important as pattern-matching what does. Brown-Séquard (crossed spinothalamic loss), isolated leg deficits, and brainstem patterns all violate the hemispheric rules — a deliberate reminder that not every deficit fits a single cortical or subcortical lesion. These are the ones to keep in your differential when the pattern doesn't add up.
